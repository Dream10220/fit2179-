01 — The big picture

What makes up Australia's
threatened species?

Plants dominate the list — but mammals face the highest proportional risk. One in three Australian mammal species is under some level of threat.

Threatened species by taxonomic group

Plants account for nearly 70% of all threatened species listings. But among mammals, the rate is alarming — roughly 1 in 3 species faces extinction. Australia has already lost more mammal species than any other continent.

03 — The endangered families

Eucalypts and orchids
lead the endangered list

The Myrtaceae family — eucalypts, the trees that define Australia — has more threatened species than any other family.

Top threatened families

EPBC-listed species per family. Green = plant, orange = animal.

Among animals, Dasyuridae (quolls and dunnarts) top the list — victims of fox and cat predation. The Potoroidae (potoroos and bettongs) have lost more than half their species range.

04 — Rising through time

The listings keep growing

Since the EPBC Act commenced in 2000, listings have accelerated. The 2019–20 bushfires triggered a wave of emergency additions.

05 — The state race

Queensland's persistent lead

A bump chart tracks state rankings over time. Tasmania has worsened dramatically — climbing from 6th to 3rd. Western Australia fluctuates as its unique flora is reassessed.

State rankings by threatened species, 2000–2024

Rank 1 = most threatened. Crossing lines = rank changes.

Tasmania's sharp rise from 6th to 3rd reflects accelerating threats to its unique island ecosystems, particularly after the 2016 and 2019 bushfire seasons devastated old-growth wilderness.

08 — The protection gap

How much of Australia
is actually protected?

Each square below represents 1% of Australia's land area. Green squares show protected land, grey shows unprotected. Only 17.9% of the continent falls within the National Reserve System.

Land protection: a waffle chart

Custom-built with derived data. Each of 100 squares = 1% of land. Green = protected, grey = unprotected.

Scientists recommend protecting 30% of land by 2030 to halt biodiversity decline. Australia is currently at 17.9% — meaning it needs to nearly double its protected area in the next few years.
